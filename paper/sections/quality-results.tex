\section{What the posterior buys}
\label{sec:quality}

% Predictive quality and calibration (Table~\ref{tab:quality}), then the

The ensemble wins predictive quality and loses calibration: the best mixture
NLPD of the four, and the worst ECE by close to a factor of two. Vadam holds
the opposite corner, worst NLPD and best ECE. Mean-field and the low-rank arm
sit between them, and by less than the two splits disagree about them, so we
do not rank those two.

Every claim in this section is read off \texttt{test}. Validation agrees on
each one; where it does not --- the middle two arms' calibration ordering
reverses between splits --- the text says so rather than picking the split
that reads better.

That trade is the most interesting thing in the table, because it is not the
usual one. The variational arms are honest about their confidence on data they
know and blind to data they do not: their ECE is low and their epistemic term
barely moves off-distribution. The ensemble is the reverse. It is
overconfident where it is right and it is the only arm whose disagreement
rises sharply where the input carries no structure at all, separating in- from
off-distribution by more than twice the margin of either variational arm
(Table~\ref{tab:ood}). One number cannot be traded for the other here; they
are measuring different failures.

A larger epistemic term is not a better posterior, and Vadam is the
demonstration. Its in-distribution epistemic is more than three times
mean-field's, while its separation ratio sits with the other variational arms
and far below the ensemble's. The extra spread is prior noise rather than
learned disagreement, for a reason that is structural and is set out below.

\paragraph{Why $S = 32$, and what the choice moved.}
Epistemic uncertainty is a Monte Carlo estimate over $S$ draws and it is
biased low until $S$ converges (Figure~\ref{fig:s-convergence}). Mean-field is
flat by $S = 32$ and confirmed flat out to $S = 128$. Under-sampling does not
merely blur the comparison, it flatters the variational arms: from $S = 8$ to
$S = 32$ their in-distribution epistemic grows faster than their
off-distribution epistemic, so their separation ratios \emph{fall} and the
ensemble's lead widens. The ensemble is pinned at $S = E$ and does not move.
Converging $S$ therefore made the paper's headline gap larger, not smaller,
which is the opposite of what a sampling correction usually does, and it is
the single number most changed by the choice.

One caveat survives the choice. The low-rank arm gains a further few percent
of epistemic between $S = 32$ and $S = 64$ and only then stops, so at $S = 32$
mean-field sits at its limit while the low-rank arm sits slightly below its
own. That asymmetry favours mean-field in a head-to-head, and it is small
against the gaps this section reads. Moving the convention to $S = 64$ would
remove it and costs no retraining.

\paragraph{Calibration degrades with $S$, in one arm.}
Mean-field's ECE rises at every doubling of $S$ from $2$ to $128$ while its
mixture NLPD improves: sharper and less honest from the same knob, with no
retraining between the two ends. The low-rank arm does not share the pattern, which
argues against a binning artifact --- if the estimator or the bin count were
responsible, both arms would show it. We did not chase it further. It does
mean that any ECE quoted for mean-field carries its $S$ or means nothing, and
that a smaller $S$ flatters it.

\paragraph{Vadam's posterior is the prior, and that is structural.}
The arm's variational parameters moved: its mean left initialisation almost
completely, and its training loss fell like the others'. Its posterior
\emph{scale} did not. Equation~\ref{eq:vadam-sigma} makes the data's
contribution to the precision $N s$, weighed against $\lambda = 2500$, so for
the data to matter at all the per-token RMS gradient has to reach
$\sqrt{\lambda / N} = 5.27 \times 10^{-3}$. The median observed value is
$2.71 \times 10^{-5}$, short by a factor of $195$.\footnote{The median is read
from the trained checkpoint by \texttt{dev/vadam\_posterior.py}; it is the one
measurement in this paper that comes from a checkpoint rather than from a
logged run. The rest of the arithmetic is $\lambda$ and $N$.}
The minibatch bias in \citeauthor{khan2018vadam}'s own Theorem~1 accounts for
at most a factor of $16$ of that at this batch size. So the posterior sits at
the prior in almost every coordinate, and the arm reports the prior's spread
as epistemic uncertainty.

This is a finding, not a defect, and it is the honest answer to what deriving
$\sigma$ from Adam's second moment buys at this scale: on a model whose
gradients are this small relative to $1/\sigma_{\text{init}}^{2}$, it buys the
prior back. The kernels were checked against a double-precision host reference
and a distributional test of the sampler, and the transcription against the
published figure, before this was concluded (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).
Rerunning the arm reproduces it, because the mechanism is $N s$ against
$\lambda$ and not an implementation error. The knob that would change it is
$\lambda$, and choosing $\lambda$ by anything other than the initialisation
would be a modelling decision this comparison does not get to make.
