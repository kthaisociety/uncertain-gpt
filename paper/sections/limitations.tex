\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

% Scope of the claim: one card, one architecture, one corpus, one scale.
% Which code paths carry a correctness argument and which do not; which
% comparisons are confounded (e.g. an arm's injected noise scale versus the
% posterior itself) and what would be needed to separate them.

\paragraph{Scale is a choice, and it is small.}
$11{,}061{,}531$ parameters on a $6$\,GB card. The scale was picked so that
all four arms fit at once: the low-rank arm and the five-member ensemble are
the memory-hungry ones, and a model large enough to fill the card would have
priced them out and destroyed the comparison. Nothing here should be read as
a claim about billion-parameter models. What transfers is the ratio between
arms on one implementation, not the absolute cost. The configuration carries
two smaller caveats. The character-level vocabulary of $27$ keeps the token
embedding negligible, which is deliberate, but it means no result here says
anything about a posterior over a large embedding table. And the position
embedding is sized for a $1024$-token context while training runs at $64$, so
about $3\%$ of the parameters never see a data gradient and are held at the
prior by the KL term alone, while still costing posterior state in three of
the four arms.

\paragraph{Nothing is converged.}
$100{,}000$ steps at $256$ tokens per step is $25.6$M tokens against a $90$M
token training split --- $28\%$ of one epoch. Every number is what a method
does at a fixed step budget, not what it converges to, and the ranking
between arms is free to change with more steps. The budget is equal across
arms, which is what the comparison needs; it is not sufficient for any arm.

\paragraph{Step times are lower bounds, not controlled measurements.}
The runs span two sessions on a card with a cooling fault. It throttled to
$46$--$51$\,W at $87$--$89$\,\textdegree C against a board budget of
$65$--$80$\,W, and the low-rank arm's step time drifted from $52$ to $65$\,ms
within a single run with no change in the work being done. A reported figure
is therefore a lower bound on what that arm actually cost per step during its
own run, and the two sessions did not throttle alike, so the arms were not
clocked under matched conditions. Read the step times as orders of magnitude
between arms. A gap of a few milliseconds is not a result. What a healthy
card of this class would deliver is not measured here at all.

\paragraph{$n = 1$, and the noise floor is unknown.}
One training run per arm. No error bars, no seed sweep. Worse, the runs are
not reproducible even in principle: three backward kernels ---
\texttt{encoder\_backward}, \texttt{layernorm\_backward} and
\texttt{attention\_backward} --- accumulate gradients with \texttt{atomicAdd},
so the order of floating-point additions varies between runs. Two identical
invocations diverge. Nothing in this paper measures how large that divergence
grows over $100{,}000$ steps, so we cannot say which differences between arms
exceed it. Small gaps should be treated as unresolved.

\paragraph{The off-distribution stream is synthetic.}
Uniform-random token ids, not shifted text. It is a direction check: sample
disagreement has to rise where the input carries no structure, and an arm
that fails it is not capturing model uncertainty at all. It is not a
distribution shift benchmark, and it supports no comparison with the
shift literature \citep{ovadia2019trust}. A second text domain would be the
minimum needed for that.

\paragraph{Noise scale and posterior quality are confounded.}
Loss currently tracks the injected noise scale monotonically across arms, so
an arm that samples less aggressively looks better on predictive metrics for
a reason that has nothing to do with the quality of its posterior. As
reported, the experiment cannot separate the two. Separating them needs a
sweep over the noise scale within each arm and a comparison of curves rather
than of single points; that runs against saved checkpoints and needs no
retraining.

\paragraph{Not every code path carries a correctness argument.}
Each kernel was validated against the CPU reference implementation, but that
reference is a single-block model, so the multi-block loop at $L = 6$ has no
independent check. Its only evidence is behavioural: the one-batch overfit
test still drives the loss to $0.0070$ at $L = 6$. A finite-difference
gradient check on a tiny multi-block configuration would close the gap and
has not been run.

\paragraph{The arms are not identical in what they cover.}
The low-rank arm places its posterior over the four GEMM weight matrices per
block and leaves embeddings, LayerNorm parameters and biases deterministic;
the other three are Bayesian over every parameter. At this configuration
that is $96\%$ of the model rather than all of it, so the gap is small, but
it is a gap and it flatters the low-rank arm's cost. Relatedly, the ensemble
evaluates at $S = E = 5$ by construction while the Bayesian arms evaluate at
whatever $S$ is requested; a mixture metric compared across arms at different
$S$ compares different sample counts, which is why $S$ is printed with every
number.
