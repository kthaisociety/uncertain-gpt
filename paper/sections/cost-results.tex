\section{What the posterior costs}
\label{sec:cost}

% Memory and time, per arm, on one card. Read Table~\ref{tab:cost}; do not
% restate its numbers in the text, interpret them. Anything a cell does not
% contain has not been measured yet.

The three posteriors price differently by an order of magnitude, and the
ordering follows the buffer count rather than the arithmetic. Vadam's step
lands within a millisecond of the ensemble member's: its posterior is optimiser
state the run had already allocated, so it adds only the sampling kernel of
Equation~\ref{eq:vadam-sigma}. Mean-field adds a second parameter-sized tensor,
its AdamW moment pair, and a second update pass. The low-rank arm adds the
factor $A$, which at $r = 8$ is eight tensors the size of the Bayesian
parameter block, read, written and sampled from every step.

The ensemble's row is one member's, and its step is the deterministic step. Its
bill is $E$ times that step and $E$ times the training run, so the cheapest arm
per step is the most expensive arm to train, and the only one whose
evaluation-time $S$ cannot be raised without paying again.

Vadam fails the one-batch overfit gate, which drives a single batch to
near-zero loss. Its injected noise does not shrink as the mean fits, so its
loss floors well above every other arm's while the run itself is healthy.
Section~\ref{sec:quality} gives the reason.

These are lower bounds, measured on a thermally limited card over two sessions
(Section~\ref{sec:limitations}). A difference of a few milliseconds between two
rows is not a result.
