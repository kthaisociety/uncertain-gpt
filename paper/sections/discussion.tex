\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

% Put cost and quality together: what the cost-per-unit-of-quality picture
% looks like once both tables are filled, which bottleneck each arm is
% actually paying, and what a practitioner should take away.

Put the two tables side by side and the ensemble is not a cheap baseline the
Bayesian arms have to beat. It is the expensive arm --- $E$ training runs ---
that beats them anyway, on predictive quality and on the one thing an
uncertainty estimate is supposed to do, which is change when the input does.
The variational arms buy their posterior inside a single training run, which
is the whole point of the literature they come from, and at this scale what
they get for it is a well-calibrated model whose epistemic term barely reacts
to uniform noise.

That is a real trade rather than a verdict. Calibration and separation are
different failures. A model that has to know when it is guessing on data it
was trained for wants the variational arms, which are honest about confidence
at a fraction of the ensemble's training bill. A model that has to notice
input it has never seen wants the ensemble, and at this scale nothing here
suggests a cheaper way to get that behaviour.

Each arm pays a different bottleneck, and the source makes it legible. The
low-rank arm pays memory: rank $8$ costs eight covariance tensors over the
Bayesian parameter block, and it buys correlation within a weight matrix and
nothing across matrices. Mean-field pays a second variational tensor and a
second optimiser pass. Vadam pays almost nothing, and at this scale gets
almost nothing --- Section~\ref{sec:quality} traces that to $N s$ against
$\lambda$ rather than to any implementation of it. The claim that a posterior
can be nearly free \citep{khan2018vadam,osawa2019practical,shen2024ivon} is
not contradicted by that: the posterior \emph{was} nearly free. It was also
nearly the prior. Whether the two can be separated at this gradient scale is
open, and the knob is $\lambda$, which we did not turn because choosing it by
anything but the initialisation is a modelling decision and not a measurement.

The most useful negative result here is about $S$. The estimator that reports
epistemic uncertainty is itself Monte Carlo, and under-sampling it does not
add noise symmetrically --- it suppresses the in-distribution term more than
the off-distribution one, so a small $S$ makes a variational posterior look
better at separation than it is. Any published ratio of this kind that does
not state its $S$ cannot be read.

\paragraph{Reproducibility.}
Everything in this paper runs from the checkpoints in the repository; no
number required a retraining run, and evaluation is deterministic and
reproduces bit for bit. The same code is packaged as a PyTorch extension: it
compiles the four arms into one translation unit, implements no kernel,
optimiser or metric of its own, and reproduces the binaries' output exactly.
Its per-step overhead measured between $-0.45\%$ and $+0.80\%$, which is
inside run-to-run spread --- the wrapper is a call path, not a second
implementation. Training is the part that does not reproduce: three backward
kernels accumulate with \texttt{atomicAdd}, so two identical runs diverge in
the last bits and then amplify (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).

\paragraph{What would change the picture.}
Three things, in the order they would be worth doing. A sweep over each arm's
injected noise scale, which runs against saved checkpoints and would separate
posterior quality from the amount of noise an arm happens to inject --- as
reported, those are confounded. A second text domain in place of the synthetic
off-distribution stream, which is the minimum for any claim that touches the
distribution-shift literature \citep{ovadia2019trust}. And more than one seed
per arm, which is what would turn the small gaps in these tables into
resolvable ones.
